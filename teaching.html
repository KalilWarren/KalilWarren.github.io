Approach

I think of myself as a teacher-scholar. Teaching is as much a part of my work as research.

What I care about most in the classroom is that students understand the concepts and can apply them to their everyday lives. I ground that understanding in hands-on practice, things students can do on their own or together in class.

I think the best learning happens when students take an active role in it. That might mean leading a discussion on a topic, working with a piece of software, or collecting their own data, making a prediction, and testing it.

I also believe in evidence-based practice. I follow the scientific literature on which teaching strategies are most effective, and I collect data on how my own students are doing. When the data point to something that could be better, I change it as I go to improve their outcomes.

StatTeacher Toolkit is the best example of this. In my statistics class I noticed that students would do every practice problem in the textbook and still not understand the topic. I turned to online test question banks, but each one had shortcomings of its own. So I decided to create StatTeacher Toolkit.

Courses as Instructor of Record

  • Spring 2027
    PSYC 455: Introduction to Neuroscience Upcoming · One section
  • Fall 2026
    PSYC 572 / LING 505: Cognitive Psychology Lab One section · In person

    A hands-on research methods course in experimental psycholinguistics. Each student designs, builds, runs, analyzes, and presents an original behavioral experiment on language. They build it in PsychoPy, make their own stimuli, analyze the data in Python or R, and present it as a research poster at the end of the semester. Real experiments often fail, so I designed the grading around the quality of each student’s reasoning and execution, not around whether the predicted effect appears.

  • Summer 2026
    PSYC 400: Survey of Learning & Memory One section · Online, asynchronous

    A survey of the cognitive science of learning and memory, taught online in a short summer session. Students run two classic memory experiments on themselves and write up what they find. Discussion prompts ask them to apply the material to their own lives and to evaluate claims about memory in popular media. Each quiz allows three attempts, which is itself retrieval practice, one of the study strategies the course teaches.

  • Fall 2025, Spring 2026
    PSYC 220: Psychological Statistics Two sections · Lecture and lab

    An introduction to the statistics used in psychological research, built around one question: “but how do we know this claim is true?” Students complete twelve lab activities where they apply each method, and they read journal articles to see how psychologists use and write about statistics. I wrote StatTeacher Toolkit for this course.

  • Spring 2024
    ENGL 102: Rhetoric and Composition Two sections

    First-year writing. Students build toward a researched paper in stages: a topic proposal, a rhetorical analysis, an annotated bibliography, and then the paper itself, with peer review along the way and a final portfolio.

  • Fall 2023
    ENGL 101: Critical Reading and Composition One section

    First-year writing. Students write a literacy narrative, a close reading essay, and a genre synthesis essay, review each other’s drafts, and put together a final portfolio.

All courses were taught at the University of South Carolina, Columbia.

StatTeacher Toolkit

The idea behind StatTeacher Toolkit is that both instructors and students can generate their own practice problems. It is a free, open-source web app that runs in the browser, so there is nothing to install and no account to create.

On the instructor side, instructors get problems they can manipulate and simulate. They choose a test, set the parameters, and get a dataset with the full results worked out. They can also turn it into a student worksheet with an answer key, or make a whole batch, and export everything to Excel. Advanced students can run the same simulations to get a deeper understanding of statistics.

Students can be sent to the student side to work practice problems and get a sense of what exam questions might look like. Each problem is new and random. Students enter their answers, get graded on each step, and can open the full solution afterward. It covers z‑tests, t‑tests, ANOVA, correlation, and regression.

I gave a talk about the project at the Psychology Department Instructor Retreat in April 2026, and a paper describing it is under review at the Journal of Open Source Education.
