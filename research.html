Research

I study how the brain builds a picture of what language describes, and how that picture breaks down.

Statement Projects Publications Presentations

Research Statement

When we understand language, we build a mental model of what is being described: who is involved, where they are, what happened. I want to know how the brain builds that model, how it holds on to it, and how it falls apart.

I come at this from three directions. In my dissertation I study where a voice came from: how listeners keep track of the locations of speakers while they follow a story. I study what breaks after a stroke, using the kinds of naming errors that people with aphasia make. And I damage language models on purpose, to see whether they make errors the way a person with aphasia does.

A newer line of work borrows methods from the study of human conversation to ask a different question: when two AI agents talk to each other, can people, or other AI systems, keep up?

Current Projects

01

Late to the Conversation: Can People and AI Monitors Follow AI–AI Conversations?

AI Safety Multi-Agent Communication Psycholinguistics LLM Evaluation

AI agents talk to each other more and more, and people can only oversee them if they can follow what is said. In human conversation, partners quickly develop shorthand that outsiders struggle to understand (Schober & Clark, 1989). I’m asking whether AI–AI conversations drift the same way, and whether people and AI monitors keep up equally well.

Pairs of multimodal AI models play a repeated visual reference game with tangram figures. Human participants and AI models then try to identify the figures as “overhearers.” Some follow the conversation from the start, some join late, and some see only isolated descriptions. Another condition puts the models under communication pressure, to test whether a push for efficiency makes AI conventions harder for people to follow than for AI monitors. The pilot is complete and human data collection is under way.

02

Spatial Memory of Auditory Sources in Language Comprehension

Spatial Cognition Psycholinguistics Auditory Processing Language Comprehension

This is my dissertation project, with Dr. Amit Almor. It asks how listeners keep track of where voices and sounds come from while they follow what is being said, and how that spatial record interacts with reference and discourse. Participants listen to stories over a ring of loudspeakers, and I measure what they remember about location and how it affects their comprehension.

Readers tie words to places on a page. I want to know whether listeners do the same with sound, which would make it a general part of how we understand discourse.

I have presented this work at Human Sentence Processing 2026 (Cambridge, MA), Psychonomics 2025 (Denver, CO), and the Graduate Students in Linguistics Student Research Symposium 2025 (Columbia, SC).

03

Error Profiles as Behavioral Biomarkers of Lesion Location

Aphasia Lesion-Symptom Mapping Neuroimaging Connected Speech

A stroke can damage the language system in many ways, and the way it fails says something about how it was put together. People with aphasia make different kinds of naming errors: a related word, a similar-sounding word, a non-word, or no answer at all. This project asks whether a person’s mix of error types holds steady across two standard tests, the Philadelphia Naming Test (PNT) and the Western Aphasia Battery-Revised (WAB-R). If it does, that mix may be a more reliable behavioral marker of where the lesion is than overall accuracy. I use lesion-symptom mapping (LSM) to test this.

The manuscript is under review at Brain Communications, with co-authors Roger Newman-Norlund, Saeed Ahmadi, Nadra Salman, Yong Yang, Regan Willis, Xiang Guan, and Julius Fridriksson.

04

LLM Perturbation Modeling of Cognitive Decline

LLM Cognitive Decline MCI Computational Modeling Discourse Analysis

Can a large language model be damaged so that it fails the way a person does? I perturb the internal parts of a model, such as attention heads, MLP layers, and the residual stream, and look for the changes in discourse that show up in aphasia and mild cognitive impairment (MCI).

I compare the model’s output with linguistic and cognitive data from the Aging Brain Cohort at the University of South Carolina (ABC@UofSC), a sample of healthy and preclinical older adults, across three kinds of discourse: picture description, story retelling, and procedural discourse. I treat cognitive decline as a continuum, using MoCA scores as a continuous measure instead of a cutoff for an MCI label.

05

Spelling and Sound in Bilingual Cognate Judgments

Bilingualism Cognates Psycholinguistics French

Cognates are words that share form and meaning across a bilingual’s two languages, like English approves and French approuve. Thirty-five English–French bilinguals listened to 255 cognate verb pairs and rated how similar the two words sounded. How similar the words were in spelling predicted the ratings. How similar they were in sound did not, even though the task was entirely auditory. Only listeners with higher French proficiency made use of sound similarity.

This is joint work with Annie Xie, Danielle Fahey, and Mila Tasseva-Kurktchieva. I presented it at the Society for the Neurobiology of Language (2026, Geneva).

06

Reference Processing Modulation During HD-tDCS

HD-tDCS Reference Processing Cognitive Neuroscience Brain Stimulation

With Sarah Wilson, Samantha Langley, Dirk-Bart den Ouden, and Amit Almor, I looked at whether high-definition transcranial direct current stimulation (HD-tDCS) of the superior parietal cortex changes how people process linguistic reference while they read (Wilson, Langley, Warren, den Ouden, & Almor, in prep). The findings were presented at the Society for the Neurobiology of Language (2023, Marseille).
